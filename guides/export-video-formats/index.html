MP4, WebM and MOV Explained: Why Video Duration Can Show 0:00

A video can play normally and still display 0:00 in a phone gallery. Often the problem is the file container's timing information, not the visible frames. This guide explains the difference and what to check before sharing an export.

Video file extension versus codec

A file name tells only part of the story. MP4 and MOV are containers that hold video, audio and metadata. H.264, HEVC and ProRes are video codecs. AAC, Opus and PCM are examples of audio formats. Two files named .mov may require entirely different decoding support.

For broad social-platform compatibility, MP4 containing H.264 video and AAC audio is a practical choice when your browser or editor supports those formats. WebM often uses VP8 or VP9 video with Opus audio. It can work well in browsers, but some mobile gallery apps do not interpret every WebM timing variation the same way.

Why a gallery might report 0:00

Some browser recording APIs write media chunks while the video is being captured. Certain recordings do not finalize the duration or indexing metadata in a form every gallery app recognizes. The file may still contain all its frames and play through to the end in a browser, even though a gallery's summary reads 0:00.

Another possible cause is a damaged or incomplete download, or an unsupported container/codec combination. Do not assume that renaming .webm to .mp4 converts the video. Renaming changes only the filename.

How to diagnose an export

  1. Check whether the file opens in a normal browser player and plays from start to finish.
  2. Compare the expected timeline duration with the playback time shown by two different players.
  3. Listen for missing sound, unexpected silence or audio drifting out of sync.
  4. Check whether the file has a genuine MP4 or WebM container, rather than trusting the extension alone.
  5. Try another short export. If only one long project fails, file size, memory and an interrupted recording may be involved.

MP4 exports from a browser

Browser media recording is not identical across Chrome, Edge, Safari and other browsers. A browser might support H.264 playback but not provide H.264 encoding through the same interface. Some workflows therefore offer a WebM fallback or a separate conversion step. The best export choice is the one your device can produce reliably and your audience can play.

ClipForge offers different export pipelines across its tools. Do not assume that a format available in one workspace is already available in every other workspace. If you are experimenting with the Studio V2 preview, treat it as a developing editor and test the specific export on your own phone before relying on it for a delivery.

What about MOV imports?

QuickTime MOV files from phones and cameras can contain H.264, HEVC, ProRes or legacy camera codecs. Some load natively in the browser. Others need remuxing, which changes the container without changing the compressed video, or transcoding, which decodes and re-encodes the media. A browser that cannot decode a codec cannot fix that limitation merely by changing the extension.

If a MOV does not import, converting a copy of it to a broadly supported H.264/AAC MP4 using a desktop editor is a practical fallback. Keep the original footage, especially if it contains higher-quality camera information you may want later.

Final upload checklist

  • Check the entire video, not only its first few seconds.
  • Confirm the gallery shows a sensible duration, or use a compatible player to investigate.
  • Check output resolution and horizontal/vertical orientation.
  • Verify voice clarity, caption placement and beginning/end cuts.
  • Store the original and a known-good rendered copy separately.
Important: Browser support is device-dependent. No video editor can guarantee that every MOV codec or gallery app will work without validation.
